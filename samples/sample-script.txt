You are seventeen years old, and the city of Pompeii has never once asked your name.

It is the morning of August 24th, and the air already tastes of salt and bread and the faint copper tang of the fish market three streets over. You wake on a straw mat in the back of your uncle's bakery. Your back aches. Your hands are cracked from the ovens. You have been awake since the fourth hour, and the dough will not knead itself.

Mr. Caelius, your uncle, is not a cruel man. He is only a tired one. "Faster," he says, not unkindly, and you are faster.

You do not know that you have eleven hours left.

The mountain behind the city is called Vesuvius, and you have never thought of it as anything but the place where the rich keep their vineyards. It is green. It is quiet. It has been quiet your whole life, and the lives of everyone you have ever met, and their parents before them. Quiet is just what mountains are.

By the sixth hour, the bread is sold. You carry the last two loaves down the Via dell'Abbondanza, past the laundry where the cloth hangs like surrendered flags, past the brothel with its painted door, past the bronze statue everyone touches for luck. You touch it too. Everyone touches it.

Her name is Livia, and she is buying figs.

You have seen her before — the daughter of a magistrate, three social classes and one impossible distance above you. She wears a pale blue stola the color of a sky that has not yet learned what it will do today. She looks at you for exactly as long as it takes to count to three, and then she looks away, and you feel the whole machinery of your small life lurch on its axis.

You are a baker's nephew. You will always be a baker's nephew. This is a fact as solid as the cobblestones under your feet.

And yet.

At the seventh hour, the first sound comes. Not loud. A low cough from somewhere underground, the way an old dog shifts in its sleep. The wine cups tremble on the counter. A dog begins to bark and does not stop. You look up at Vesuvius and the mountain looks exactly as it always has, green and patient, and you tell yourself it was nothing.

The truth is not clean, and the truth is this: the mountain has been lying to your city for three hundred years.

At the first hour past noon, Vesuvius opens.

There is no warning anyone is willing to believe. A column of white rushes into the sky, straight up, taller than anything you have words for, and it stands there like a pine tree made of smoke. The whole forum stops. Eleven thousand people tilt their heads back at once. Someone laughs, because what else do you do. Someone screams, because what else do you do.

You think of Livia. You do not know why. You think of the blue of her dress against the grey that is now eating the sun.

Ash begins to fall like a snow that has never heard of winter.

It is soft at first. Children catch it on their tongues. Then it thickens, and the pieces grow teeth — small stones, light as walnuts, then heavier, drumming on the terracotta roofs, on shoulders, on the upturned faces of people who have not yet decided to be afraid. You pull your tunic over your head. You run.

Here is what the ash does. It falls, and it falls, and it does not stop falling, and within an hour it is to your ankles, and the rich are loading carts they will never outrun, and the poor are praying to gods who are also, you understand now, made only of stone.

You should run to the harbor. Everyone is running to the harbor.

You run the other way.

You run toward the magistrate's house on the hill, against the current of the whole dying city, because of a girl who counted to three. It makes no sense. It is the first decision in your life that has been entirely, recklessly your own.

The door is open. The house is empty. The figs are still on the table, and one has rolled onto the floor, and you stand in a stranger's atrium with stone falling through the open roof and you understand that she is already gone — that the wealthy left first, that they always leave first, that the distance between you was real until the exact moment it stopped mattering.

Control is the illusion the comfortable can afford. You were never comfortable. So you were never fooled.

You walk back out into it.

The sky is black now, at what should be afternoon. You cannot tell the ash from the dark. Lamps are lit in windows by people who have decided to wait, and you want to tell them, and you do not, because there is nothing to say that the mountain is not already saying louder.

By the temple of Isis you find Mr. Caelius. He has fallen. His leg is pinned beneath a column that used to hold up something beautiful. "Go," he says. The ash is in his beard, in the lines of his face, turning him into his own statue while he still breathes. "Go, you idiot boy. Go."

You do not go.

You dig. Your cracked hands, the ones the ovens ruined, find the strength for this one thing, and you lift, and you pull, and you drag the man who fed you out from under the weight of a god's house, and the two of you stumble toward a sea you cannot see.

Pliny the Elder, the admiral, is bringing ships across the bay right now to save people exactly like you. He will not make it in time. You do not know his name and he does not know yours, and this is how it has always been between the powerful and the rest — they arrive, when they arrive, too late and full of good intentions.

The last thing you see clearly is the bronze statue at the crossroads, the lucky one, the one everyone touches. A child is clinging to it. Still touching it. Still hoping it works.

You take the child's hand. You take Mr. Caelius's arm. You walk into the dark toward the water, three people the city never named, and the ash closes over the street behind you like a page turning in a book no one will read for seventeen hundred years.

They will find you in 1748. They will pour plaster into the hollow your body leaves in the rock, and they will marvel at the shape of you — a young person, reaching, holding on to two others, frozen in the one act that made an entire life mean something.

So here is the only question that matters, and I want you to actually answer it.

When the mountain finally opens — and for every one of us, it does — will you run toward the harbor with everyone else?

Or will you run the other way, toward the person who only ever counted to three?
